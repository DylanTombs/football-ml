# Fix the pick rule before the model

The biggest, cheapest gain for our weekly game comes from the **decision layer**, not from a better Dixon-Coles fit. Over the 90 backtest matches (GW1–9 2026-27) the model's expected goals (Σ λ+μ) total **256 against 243 actual**, so its goal rates are roughly calibrated and slightly high. The headline scorelines total only **188 goals** because we pick the modal scoreline. Under our rules every predicted goal is a free scorer pick, worth about 0.1–0.6 expected points, while the outcome points given up by a slightly less likely scoreline are only about 0.02–0.06 per extra goal. An expected-points (EP) pick rule restricted to plausible scorelines (P ≥ ~5%) is worth roughly **+0.4 to +0.8 EP per match in stylised calculations**. A rebuilt scorer model (npxG share × team λ × expected minutes, penalty takers, exact repeat-pick maths) and fixed rather than sampled goal minutes (about +4.5 pts per 90 matches) add smaller, solid gains. Feeding de-margined bookmaker odds into λ/μ is the best-evidenced way to improve the probabilities themselves. An LLM is worth adding only as a schema-bound fact extractor whose effects are fitted and shrunk toward zero, and it can only be judged in frozen forward tests. There is a tension: taken to its limit, our scoring rewards predicting 6-5 in every match, which would make a public "how good is our model" series look silly. Dylan needs to decide whether to cap scorelines or change the rules. Finally, 90 matches carry a standard error of about **±13 points on a 141-point total**, so plan 02's conclusion (an injury cut lost 3–18 points) is weak evidence, and every change from here needs paired, multi-season testing.

## The 188-vs-256 gap comes from picking modal scorelines, not from the goal rates

The modal-scoreline effect is mechanical. For a Poisson rate of 1.3–1.6 the mode is 1 while the mean is 1.3–1.6, so a perfectly calibrated model that reports its most likely score will show about 2.0–2.2 goals per match. The team-strength research predicted exactly this ([Premier League / Opta context in team-strength notes](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4580269/why-more-late-goals-are-being-scored-in-2025-26-premier-league-season)), and the repo measurement (256 expected vs 188 headline vs 243 actual) confirms it. Earlier notes that call the model "under-predicting goals" are wrong. A total-goals multiplier or a faster-decaying intercept would push the rates *further* above reality and should not be built to close this gap.

The cost shows up in the scoring formula. For a pick (a,b) with result r, outcome EP = P(r) + 2·P(a,b). Scorer EP is the sum, over the a+b picks, of each pick's match probability q(k), and minute EP adds q(k)·h, where h ≈ 0.21–0.28 is the chance of landing within ±10 minutes given a hit. Every q(k) is positive and there is no penalty for a wrong scorer, so **total EP rises strictly with every extra predicted goal**. In a Dixon-Coles grid (ρ = −0.1, stylised player shares), the unconstrained optimum is 6-5 or 6-6 for every matchup tried, adding about +1.3 to +1.6 EP per match over the modal pick (own calculation, `research_notes/.../expected_points_and_timing.md`). The method itself is standard: enumerate the score matrix and take the argmax of expected points, as a 2026 World Cup Dixon-Coles engine under the same 3/1 outcome rule did ([sakthiish.com, Jul 2026](https://www.sakthiish.com/blog/2026-07-14-world-cup-prediction-engine)).

A constrained search keeps most of that gain while staying believable. Restricting candidates to scorelines with P(a,b) ≥ 6% moves typical picks one goal up (1-0 → 2-1, 2-0 → 3-1, 0-1 → 1-2) and raised total EP from 1.06 to 1.62 in one illustrative fixture, at a cost of only 0.03–0.11 outcome EP. At a 4% threshold picks drift to 3-1 and 2-2, and at 2% to 3-2 and 4-1. The **+0.4 to +0.8 EP per match** these grids show depends on the assumed player shares. If real depth-player probabilities are lower, the gain shrinks, so the backtest has to measure it with a `--pick-strategy` switch. A simpler estimate of lifting predicted goals from 188 toward 243 (about 55 extra picks at marginal hit rates of 0.12–0.20) gives **+5 to +12 points per 90 matches**. That is a safer planning figure.

On the outcome term alone, EP-optimal picking barely matters. Across 315 simulated matchups, the true outcome argmax differed from "likeliest result, then likeliest score" in only 17 cases, worth about 0.2% of outcome EP. Nearly all were extra 1-1 picks in even games, which raised draw picks from 8 to 24 of 315 (own calculation). Picking draws only when they are the best bet, about 5–10% of fixtures, is correct even though about a quarter of matches end level ([buli-tipphilfe.de](https://buli-tipphilfe.de/kicktipp-strategie)). Once scorer EP is included, draws move from 1-1 to 2-2.

### The content needs a plausibility rule, and possibly a rule change

This is a choice for Dylan, not a modelling result. A model that predicts 6-5 would beat a sensible one on points while looking absurd on screen. Three options keep the series credible. First, **cap plausibility**: only allow scorelines with P ≥ ~5%, or cap total goals at about 4, and say so on screen. Second, **charge a penalty per wrong scorer** (e.g. −0.5), so a pick is only worth making when q·(1+h) > penalty·(1−q). Third, **fix the number of scorer picks** (e.g. three per match, or score scorers by precision). Options two and three remove the degeneracy, so "how many goals" becomes a calibration question again and the modal-vs-mean gap stops mattering for scorers. Any rule change breaks comparability with the 141-point baseline, so rescore the baseline under the new rules when making it.

## Two near-free tidy-ups: fixed goal minutes and exact repeat-pick maths

Sampling minutes from the season's goal-time distribution is beaten by always using the densest window. On the elapsed scale (45+2 → 47) used in `evaluate/points.py`, first-half stoppage folds into minutes 46–50 next to early second-half goals. The ±10 window centred near **minute 50–58 catches about 28% of goals**, while a random draw averages **21.3%**. That matches the backtest's 15 minute hits from 67 scorer hits (22.4%). Fixing the minute is worth about **67 × (0.28 − 0.213) ≈ +4.5 points per 90 matches** in sample (own calculation on cached ESPN data). The robust finding is the 45–60 plateau, not the exact peak. Late goals spread across 85–105 because added time varies, which makes "90" a worse centre than it sounds, even though stoppage-time goals reached record shares of 11–13% recently ([Premier League / Opta, Feb 2026](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4580269/why-more-late-goals-are-being-scored-in-2025-26-premier-league-season)). Different players' minutes don't compete, so each can use the best minute. Repeat picks of one player should tile non-overlapping windows (39 & 61; 39, 61 & 83). For out-of-sample use, fit the curve on prior seasons or on a smoothed shape: a flat first half, a linear second-half rise ([Mingotti, arXiv 1408.5442](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1408.5442)) and a stoppage spike. Spreading minutes purely to look natural costs about 0.07 points per hit.

The current "decay for already-picked players" heuristic should be replaced by exact probability. If player goals are Poisson (thinning a Poisson team total, [arXiv 2105.09881](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.09881)), the k-th pick of the same player is worth exactly P(X ≥ k). The objective is then separable and concave, so greedy selection on that marginal gain is optimal. A repeat beats the next distinct player only when P(top scores ≥ 2) > P(next scores ≥ 1). For a striker with μ = 0.875 that requires the next option to have μ below 0.25, which only happens for Haaland-type shares on high-λ teams once three or more picks are needed.

## Better scorer probabilities come from npxG, minutes and penalties

The current scorer rule ranks players by season-to-date 0.7·xG + 0.3·goals. It hits **35.6% per pick (67/188)**, about the anytime probability of a typical starting forward. The literature points to three fixes.

**Use shrunk non-penalty xG, not goals.** Finishing skill (goals/xG) persists only about 0.1 year to year even over 60+ shots, while xG/90 persists about 0.6 over 30+ matches, or 0.4 for players who change team ([Evidently](https://evidently.substack.com/p/how-can-we-tell-if-a-footballer-will)). FPL Review's simulation finds xG/90 estimates "highly distorted" under 10 matches and recommends 20–40. Forty matches of xG gives the confidence of 100 matches of goals ([FPL Review](https://docs.fplreview.com/articles/xg-accuracy/)). Shrink each player's npxG/90 toward a positional prior with k ≈ 10–20 matches, and lean on last season's rate in GW1–5.

**Model minutes explicitly.** Minutes are the main factor that FPL-only models get wrong. OpenFPL, which has no start information, trails FPL Review mostly on zero-minute predictions (RMSE 0.818 vs 0.689) ([OpenFPL, arXiv 2508.09992](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09992v1)). Even commercial predicted line-ups get only about 85–88% of starters right ([CMOtech UK](https://cmotech.uk/story/flashscore-claims-top-accuracy-in-predicted-line-ups); [fpl.page](https://fpl.page/article/fpl-gw23-predicted-lineups-team-news-2526)). So the realistic gain is zeroing out injured or suspended players and down-weighting rotation risks. Use P(start) from the last five available gameweeks × `chance_of_playing_next_round`, and expected minutes ≈ P(start)·~80 + P(sub)·~20.

**Handle penalties separately.** A penalty is worth about 0.78 xG ([jobsinfootball](https://jobsinfootball.com/blog/what-is-the-xg-of-a-penalty/)), and PL conversion has run 80–90% ([The Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/premier-league-penalties-like-free-goal)). Giving the `penalties_order == 1` taker an additive term (team pens per match × 0.78) is worth roughly +0.08–0.12 to μ on a typical team.

The resulting model is μ_i = λ_team × npshare_i × (expected minutes_i / 90) + pen term, with P(score) = 1 − e^(−μ). Shares are renormalised per team with a ~3–4% own-goal and "other" bucket. Scaling by the match model's λ ties scorer picks to the score model, so improving λ (for example with odds) improves both. One leakage trap matters here: `bootstrap-static` season totals, `penalties_order`, `news` and `chance_of_playing` are snapshots of now. The backtest must rebuild player state from `element-summary` `history` rows with kickoff before the fixture. Historical availability flags cannot be reconstructed, so **start archiving `bootstrap-static` before every gameweek now**. The vaastav dataset (MIT) has per-fixture xG and starts from 2022-23, which allows a roughly four-season scorer backtest, but it has no goal minutes ([vaastav/Fantasy-Premier-League](https://github.com/vaastav/Fantasy-Premier-League)).

## Bookmaker odds are the best input for λ and μ

De-margined odds are consistently the most accurate public 1X2 forecast ([Egidi, Pauli & Torelli, arXiv 1802.08848](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08848)). In the one study with exact figures, a Bayesian Poisson model gave the observed EPL result an average probability of **0.4349 against 0.4516 for Shin-adjusted odds**, the largest gap of four leagues ([arXiv 1802.08848 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08848)). A blend y ~ Poisson(p·θ_model + (1−p)·λ_market) found the market carried information "comparable" to historical results, but the blend still did not beat the odds. The repo data shows how big this lever might be. A naive "market favourite wins 1-0" rule scored **0.70–0.83 outcome points per match across five seasons**, against the baseline's 0.656 on GW1–9 (computed from cached [football-data.co.uk](https://football-data.co.uk/mmz4281/2425/E0.csv) CSVs). The 90-match sample cannot settle it, but that naive rule belongs in every backtest as a benchmark. A gap of 0.05–0.15 points per match would be worth **+4 to +14 points per 90 matches** on outcomes alone. The scorer picks would gain on top, through better λ.

The recipe uses data we already have. First, de-margin 1X2 with Shin or the power method rather than basic normalisation ([implied vignette](https://cran.uni-muenster.de/web/packages/implied/vignettes/introduction.html); Shin preferred per Štrumbelj, cited in [Egidi et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.08848)). Then fit (λ, μ, optionally ρ) so the Dixon-Coles grid reproduces P(H), P(D), P(A) and P(over 2.5). penaltyblog's `goal_expectancy` does this out of the box ([penaltyblog docs](https://penaltyblog.readthedocs.io/en/master/models/goal_expectancy.html)). Finally, blend rates as λ = w·λ_model + (1−w)·λ_market, with w chosen by backtest; expect a small w. For honesty, use only the **pre-closing (non-`C`) columns**, which are collected Friday afternoon for weekend games and Tuesday for midweek games ([football-data notes](https://www.football-data.co.uk/notes.txt)). The live pipeline reads `fixtures.csv` at predict time and stores the odds alongside the frozen prediction ([football-data matches](https://www.football-data.co.uk/matches.php)). Closing odds stay as a benchmark only: Pinnacle closing RPS was 0.18–0.20 per EPL season, and the market-average closing RPS on our 90 matches was 0.2058 ([pena.lt](https://pena.lt/y/2025/07/16/how-accurate-are-soccer-odds/); repo computation). One trade-off for the content: an odds-anchored model is partly "the market's model". The series should say so rather than imply the edge is home-grown.

Changes to the Dixon-Coles structure rank below this. Among count models, published RPS differences are third- or fourth-decimal, and no family reliably beats Dixon-Coles ([Bunker, Yeung & Fujii 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07669); [arXiv 2508.20075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.20075)). The better-supported structural fixes are about inputs and drift. Shots and corners carry more information than goals as rating inputs ([Wheatcroft, arXiv 2001.09097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09097)), and the shots columns are already in the cached CSVs. Home advantage is at a record low: 36% home wins after 50 matches of 2026-27 ([Premier League / Opta, Oct 2026](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/4729254/more-comebacks-and-quicker-restarts-five-trends-from-202627-so-far)). That argues for a per-season or faster-decayed home parameter, plus separate PL and Championship intercepts so Championship scoring doesn't distort the PL rate. These mainly fix outcome and draw calibration. With rates already about 5% high, a quick check that mean predicted λ equals observed goals per league-season is enough on the goal-rate side. Retuning ξ is unlikely to matter much, because published optimal half-lives from 6 months to 3 years give near-identical RPS ([arXiv 1806.03208](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.03208)).

## An LLM should extract facts, not set multipliers

The 2026 World Cup benchmarks are consistently negative for LLMs as forecasters. Six frontier models with web search matched "back the bookmaker favourite" at 63.9%, mostly by doing exactly that. They **under-committed to draws and goals** and clustered on one stock scoreline ([WorldCup Arena, arXiv 2608.04008](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04008)). Four agents, including Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5, failed to beat the market's Brier score, and agreed on the top pick 92% of the time ([arXiv 2607.17765](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17765)). Web access improved Brier by only 0.023 ([arXiv 2607.24573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24573)). An LLM that sets λ directly would bring those biases straight into the pipeline.

The useful role is narrower. Team news does carry signal. Lineup-based player ratings combined with team ratings beat either alone ([Arntzen & Hvattum 2021](https://statmod.org/smij/Vol21/Iss5/Arntzen/Abstract.html)), and betting markets react sluggishly at first to absence announcements ([Fischer & Schmal](https://www.tu-ilmenau.de/universitaet/fakultaeten/fakultaet-wirtschaftswissenschaften-und-medien/aktuelles/news/einzelnachricht/pricing-in-response-to-new-information-the-case-of-betting-markets)). But teams adapt: shots and goals *rose* while players were away at tournaments ([Universidade de Évora 2025](https://rdpc.uevora.pt/handle/10174/39051)). The judgemental-forecasting literature adds that adjustments help when they are **large, rare and based on genuinely new information**, and hurt when small and frequent. Statistically debiasing the adjustments is the most effective fix ([Fildes, Goodwin & De Baets 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v41y2025i2p649-669.html); [Fildes et al. 2009](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/45487/)).

That evidence points to a specific design:

1. **Extract facts into a schema.** The LLM reads snapshotted text (FPL `news`, one preview per fixture) and fills a fixed schema per team: players out or doubtful (validated against the FPL squad list), whether each is a regular starter, days out, players returning, a new manager, a European game within three days, and dead-rubber status.
2. **Fit the magnitudes.** A fitted model, not the LLM, turns those features into log-λ offsets, regularised toward zero.
3. **Only react to news.** Offsets fire only on *changes* since the rating window; long absences are already in the results.
4. **Keep it symmetric and bounded.** Opponent defensive absences raise λ, and returns count too. Clamp offsets to about ±15% and re-centre so the gameweek mean is about 1.

Build the non-LLM version first from archived FPL flags with replacement-adjusted shares. Many of these features need no LLM at all. Plan 02 also fits this picture. Cutting λ by the missing players' raw xG share ignored replacements, and under modal picking any λ cut also removes scorer picks. Its −3 to −18 result is within noise, though, so the idea is untested rather than refuted.

LLM adjustments cannot be backtested honestly. Models leak post-cutoff knowledge even when told to ignore it, and the leak shows in the forecasts even when the reasoning reads clean ([Simulated Ignorance Fails, arXiv 2601.13717](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13717v1); [arXiv 2506.00723](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.00723v1)). The only valid test is a frozen **shadow-mode forward test**: each gameweek, freeze both the baseline and the adjusted predictions, pin the model version, and log prompts and outputs. At 10 matches a gameweek, a Brier-scale effect of about 0.02 needs most of a season before it can be told from noise. So the LLM layer is a long-running experiment, not a quick win.

## Ninety matches cannot rank these ideas, so build the test bench first

Per-match points have an SD of about 1.36, so the 141-point baseline carries a **standard error of about ±13 points**. Even two near-identical variants (per-match correlation 0.9) need a **16-point** difference over 90 matches to be detected at 80% power (computed from `out/backtest/results`; DM test per [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/dev/generated/statsmodels.tsa.stattools.diebold_mariano_test.html)). Plan 02's 3–18 point loss sits inside that band. Choose score-model settings on out-of-sample **exact-scoreline log loss** over 2021-22 to 2024-25 walk-forward refits (about 1,520 matches). Log loss picks the better forecaster more reliably than RPS ([Wheatcroft 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/jqsprt/v17y2021i4p273-287n1.html); [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.08980)). Then confirm once on 2025-26 as a holdout, and report points only as a paired block-bootstrap difference over gameweeks against the baseline, together with the number of matches whose prediction changed. Judge decision-layer changes (pick rule, minutes, scorers) on points, since they have no probability to score, but split them by component so that scorer noise (SD 0.78) does not hide outcome changes. A cautionary example from a practitioner: adding squad-quality features cut backtest points from 96 to 86 ([sakthiish.com](https://www.sakthiish.com/blog/2026-07-14-world-cup-prediction-engine)).

The recommended order sequences these so that each plan can be judged by the one before it. **Plan 04** should be the decision layer: settle the rules question with Dylan first, then implement the EP pick with a plausibility floor, fixed minutes and exact repeat-pick maths behind a `--pick-strategy` switch. Add paired-bootstrap reporting and the naive-favourite benchmark to `backtest` in the same plan, and start the `bootstrap-static` archive that day, because those snapshots cannot be recovered later. **Plan 05** rebuilds the scorer model with leak-free player state, backtested on vaastav 2022-23 onwards. **Plan 06** extends the backtest across seasons with log-loss tuning, then adds the odds blend, choosing w on the tuning seasons and confirming on the 2025-26 holdout. **Plan 07** covers the structural Dixon-Coles tweaks, judged on log loss. **Plan 08** builds availability features from the archived flags and then, in shadow mode for the rest of the season, the LLM extractor on top. The LLM layer comes last because it is the least evidenced. Its value is the one result this project can produce that no published study yet has.

## Conclusion

The research changes where effort should go. Our scoring rule makes the scoreline pick an inventory decision about how many scorer lottery tickets to buy, and the current modal pick buys about a quarter too few. More points will come from better decisions on top of the probabilities we already have, and from market information, than from new model families or LLM judgement. The same analysis shows that maximising points without limits would produce scorelines a viewer would laugh at. The strongest version of the content series is open about this: "our model, constrained to plausible scores, anchored partly to the market, with an LLM tested in shadow mode". It also means accepting that any single season's points total, ours or a rival's, is mostly noise.
